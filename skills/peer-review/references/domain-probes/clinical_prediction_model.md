# Clinical Prediction-Model probes (CP1–CP8)

An 8-probe checklist for **cross-sectional / observational clinical prediction models** — a binary or multinomial outcome predicted from a covariate set, reported under TRIPOD / TRIPOD+AI, usually as a **nested predictor-set comparison** ("does adding marker X to a base model improve prediction of Y?"). This is the diagnostic/prognostic *prediction* counterpart to `survival_prognostic.md` (time-to-event) and complements the `observational_confounding.md` probes (a prediction model still has an analysis unit (O8) and can be over-adjusted (O7)). Route here when the manuscript develops or compares prediction models and reports discrimination (AUC/C-statistic), calibration, or decision-curve / net-benefit analysis.

**CP1 — Apparent vs optimism-corrected performance**:
- Discrimination, **calibration** (slope / intercept / calibration plot), and **decision-curve / net-benefit** all suffer optimism when computed in the same sample used to fit the model. A manuscript that optimism-corrects *only* the C-statistic (bootstrap / cross-validation) but reports calibration and net benefit **in-sample** has corrected one axis and left two apparent.
- An apparent calibration slope near unity (≈ 1.0) does **not** establish out-of-sample calibration — it is the expected in-sample result and says little about transportability.
- Calibration and DCA computed in-sample must be **labelled "apparent"**, or the optimism correction must be extended to them (e.g. bootstrap the calibration slope/intercept and the net-benefit curves). In-sample calibration/DCA described as "well calibrated" / "clinically useful" without the apparent caveat → MAJOR.
- A non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test does not establish calibration: it depends on sample size and grouping and says nothing about the direction or size of miscalibration. Ask for a calibration curve with its uncertainty, plus calibration intercept and slope, each labelled apparent or validated. In a small cohort a smoothed curve with wide uncertainty and an honest caveat is enough; do not demand every measure. Cross-link `survival_prognostic.md` S7 / T2.

**CP2 — The two-null distinction (incremental value vs marginal effect)**:
- A "negative" prediction study conflates two different nulls that have different evidentiary status:
  - **Incremental value negligible** — a *well-powered* statement: ΔAUC ≈ 0, optimism correction does not favour the larger model (and may reverse sign toward the parsimonious one), net benefit does not exceed the base model. It supports "adding X does not improve prediction" only when the uncertainty (the CI of ΔAUC or of the net-benefit difference) excludes a clinically meaningful gain; otherwise the supportable wording is "no improvement was detected".
  - **Marginal predictor effect null** — an *underpowered* statement: the predictor's adjusted OR/coefficient has a confidence interval that still admits a clinically relevant effect (e.g. OR up to ~1.7). This is "we could not exclude an effect," not "there is no effect."
- A manuscript that collapses both into a blanket "X did not predict Y" overstates the marginal-effect arm. Flag when a negative conclusion rests on a non-significant marginal OR whose CI admits a relevant effect, without separating it from the (better-powered) incremental-value finding → MAJOR. The honest reframe reports both explicitly.

**CP3 — Events-per-variable (EPV) per nested model**:
- Report events per candidate parameter for **each** nested model, not just the full cohort, counting every candidate parameter considered (dummy levels, non-linear terms, variables screened out), not only those retained. EPV is a rough screen, not a sufficiency rule: whether the sample supports a model depends on the number of candidate parameters, the outcome proportion and the expected model performance (sample-size criteria for model development: Riley et al., *BMJ* 2020;368:m441). The same 6 covariates fitted in an age≥60 subgroup with 13 events (EPV ≈ 2) remain separation-prone and unstable.
- Penalization (Firth / ridge / LASSO) reduces overfitting and should be stated, but it does not rescue a sample that is too small for the candidate set, and the amount of shrinkage is itself unstable in small samples. For subgroup or interaction models with EPV in the low single digits, assess separation, the estimation method, the uncertainty and the model complexity; unstable estimates should be reported as exploratory with their intervals, and no reliable effect should be inferred from them.
- Low EPV triggers an assessment of the sample-size justification, separation, uncertainty and validation. MAJOR when demonstrated instability undermines the claim (for example, subgroup or interaction effects presented as reliably estimated); request clarification when adequacy cannot be assessed from what is reported.

**CP4 — Net benefit is a model comparison, not a policy endorsement**:
- A decision curve showing the model exceeds treat-all / treat-none across a threshold range is a **model-vs-default** statement about ranking, not evidence that the underlying **imaging / test / screening strategy** should be adopted in practice. Net benefit is computed on the same outcomes and says nothing about cost, downstream harm, or whether the test should be ordered.
- A conclusion that reads decision-curve superiority as an endorsement of the clinical strategy ("supports using X for screening / triage") → MAJOR (scope; cross-link `scope-coherence-gate.md`). Reframe to the model-comparison claim the DCA actually supports.

**CP5 — Intended-use horizon leakage (claim-timepoint vs feature availability)**:
- Read the intended-use horizon off the title / abstract / aim — the adjectives **preoperative, pretreatment, baseline, screening, triage, pre-procedure** assert a decision made *before* some event X. Then check each predictor's **availability timepoint**: any feature that exists only *after* X (post-procedure pathology, treatment-response variables, post-contrast-only measures in a "pre-contrast screening" claim, follow-up-derived labels) is leakage relative to the claimed use, even if it is not train/test leakage.
- This is a **claim × feature-availability mismatch**: the model may be statistically clean yet unusable at the moment its claim targets. Distinct from ordinary data leakage (which is about train/test contamination) — this keys on the decision horizon.
- Flag the specific offending predictor(s) and either narrow the intended-use claim to the horizon at which all inputs exist, or drop the post-horizon features and re-fit. → MAJOR when a post-horizon variable supports a pre-horizon decision claim.

**CP6 — Validation-nomenclature conflation (development step vs test step)**:
- Distinguish the **development** step (including any internal cross-validation or bootstrap optimism correction) from a **held-out / external test** step. Flag phrasing that merges them: "developed with external validation" where a single external set is used *as* development data; "trained and validated on the external cohort"; an "external validation set" that was used for tuning/feature selection.
- Bootstrap optimism correction and ordinary within-population cross-validation are legitimate **internal validation** when every development step (variable selection, tuning, threshold choice) is repeated inside each resample; they estimate optimism in the development population and are **not** evidence of transportability. Internal–external cross-validation that holds out whole centres or studies does speak to performance across settings, limited to the settings represented. Proper internal validation does support qualified conclusions about new patients from the development population. The overclaim is presenting it as external validation or as evidence of transportability to untested populations or settings ("externally validated", "generalisable across centres", "ready for clinical use" with no external data); ask for "internally validated" and a clear description of the resampling workflow. Resampling only to count how often each variable is selected measures selection **stability**, not performance, and is not validation. Require an explicit statement of which data trained and which tuned, and, when a held-out or external set is claimed, that it did not influence model fitting, selection, tuning or thresholds (pre-specified evaluation analyses and uncertainty estimates on the frozen model are fine).
- → MAJOR when the overclaim props up a generalisability/deployment-readiness conclusion; a clarify-request when the data flow is actually clean but the wording is loose.

**CP7 — Prediction claim, association evidence (entry check)**:
- Decide first whether the paper asks an **association** question (is X independently related to Y after adjustment?) or a **prediction** question (how accurately can risk be estimated for a new patient?). A significant adjusted OR or HR does not show useful prediction, and a model built for prediction does not license independent-effect or causal language for its coefficients.
- Flag a prediction claim ("predicts", "can be used to predict", "a predictive marker") supported only by association statistics with no discrimination, calibration or validation, and the reverse: causal interpretations or inferential claims about predictor effects that the design does not support or that ignore selection and shrinkage (describing how a predictor moves the fitted prediction is fine). A paper that states both objectives separately and analyses each appropriately is fine.
- Severity: MAJOR when the headline prediction claim rests on association statistics alone. Cross-link CP2 (two nulls), CP4, and the task-formulation audit.

**CP8 — How the predictors were chosen and handled, and whether the reported model is the fitted one**:
- The common shortcuts fail in different ways. **Univariable P-value screening** discards predictors that matter only jointly and keeps chance associations. **Stepwise or other automated selection** gives unstable models and biased coefficients; repeating the whole selection procedure inside internal validation is needed for an honest performance estimate, though it does not remove the instability or the coefficient bias. **Dichotomising a continuous predictor** at a data-derived "optimal" cut-off loses information and inflates apparent performance. **Complete-case analysis**, or excluding patients whose outcome or predictor could not be obtained, reduces the sample and may introduce selection bias or restrict applicability, depending on the missingness mechanism, who was excluded and the intended deployment population. Ask what was done and whether internal validation repeated it.
- Severity follows the consequence, not the label; disclosure or penalization does not by itself make the finding MINOR. MAJOR when selection or categorisation is data-driven, is not repeated within the internal-validation resamples, the frozen pipeline has no independent evaluation, and the performance claim depends on it; MINOR when it is disclosed and its effect is shown to be small. Evaluating the frozen model on untouched external data is valid without repeating the selection there.
- Check that the model in the paper is the model that was fitted: the variables and coefficients of the final model (or its equation or score) match the multivariable results table, and any re-fitting step between the two is described. Keep an explanatory adjusted-OR table distinct from the predictive equation. A table–model mismatch that changes which predictors the model uses → MAJOR.
- Reporting completeness is not low risk of bias: a fully ticked TRIPOD or TRIPOD+AI checklist can describe a high-risk design. Judge risk of bias separately (PROBAST or its AI extension), and check that the checklist's page references point to text that exists.
- Cross-link `observational_confounding.md` O2 (adjustment-set selection in causal models, a different reason), O5 (missing data), O12 (data-driven thresholds), `model_development.md` MD1 / MD2 / MD7, `survival_prognostic.md` T4, CP3.

**Output template (CP1 / CP2 example)**:
> "Discrimination is bootstrap optimism-corrected, but the calibration slope (0.99) and the net-benefit curves are computed in the development sample and described as 'well calibrated' and 'clinically useful' without the apparent caveat — an in-sample slope near 1.0 is expected and does not establish out-of-sample calibration. I'd suggest labelling the calibration and decision-curve results 'apparent' or extending the optimism correction to them. Relatedly, the Conclusion states that the marker 'did not predict' the outcome, but two distinct results are merged: the incremental-value estimate is near zero (ΔAUC ≈ 0, optimism correction favours the parsimonious model), but its CI must be examined before a meaningful gain can be excluded, whereas the marginal adjusted OR has a confidence interval that still admits an effect up to ~1.7 (inconclusive). I'd report these separately — 'adding the marker did not meaningfully improve discrimination as measured by AUC' is supported if the CI of ΔAUC excludes a meaningful gain (calibration and net benefit need their own assessment before a broader claim); 'the marker has no association' is not."
